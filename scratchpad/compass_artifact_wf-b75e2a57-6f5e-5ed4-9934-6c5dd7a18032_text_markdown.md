# Designing a Presenting-Complaint Pick-List for Rural PHC Intake in the MP Tribal Belt: An Evidence-Grounded Starting Point

## TL;DR
- The evidence strongly supports a fixed ~30-item, vernacular-first pick-list built on a small number of high-frequency symptoms — fever, cough/cold, body/joint pain, abdominal pain, diarrhoea, weakness, headache, dizziness, and "gas/acidity" — which together account for the majority of rural Indian PHC visits; ICPC-2 (the WHO Reason-for-Encounter classification) is the best-fit backbone, with IMNCI and antenatal danger-sign lists supplying the paediatric and maternal clusters.
- For the Dhar/Jhabua/Barwani tribal belt you MUST add locally endemic conditions that national averages under-represent: malaria/fever, sickle cell pain crisis, severe anaemia, malnutrition, and snake/scorpion bite — Madhya Pradesh has India's highest sickle cell load (an estimated 961,492 sickle heterozygotes and 67,861 homozygotes; 27 of 45 districts in the gene belt with HbS prevalence 10–33%, per Colah et al., *Indian Journal of Medical Research* 2015), and MP child anaemia reached 72.7% in NFHS-5 (2019–21).
- Labels should be vernacular-first (Hindi/Malvi idiom shown to the worker, English clinical term + ICPC-2 code stored as metadata), because forcing a non-clinical ASHA/nurse to map an idiom like *kamzori* or *garmi* onto a clinical category is a clinical-salience judgment they should not silently make; a mandatory "Other — describe" free-text path must catch the long tail, and the whole list must be validated against real local PHC register data before deployment.

## Key Findings

**1. A short list covers most visits.** Across Indian primary-care studies, a handful of symptoms dominate. In a first-of-its-kind Indian study using the ICPC classification across Odisha public facilities (3,044 patients), "the most common reasons to encounter health facilities were fever, hypertension, abdominal pain, chest pain, arthritis, skin disease, cough, diabetes, and injury," and 65% of patients reported a *symptom* (not a diagnosis) as their reason for coming. ICPC-2 reason-for-encounter studies repeatedly show the top ~30 codes cover roughly 80% of all encounters — direct evidence that a ~30-item pick-list is viable.

**2. Central-India/MP data confirm the ranking but shift it toward infections and nutrition.** The AIIMS Bhopal Centre for Urban Health study (Gupta et al., N=6,685 new episodes, 2014) found respiratory disorders 27.2% (acute respiratory infection alone 23.9%), digestive 10.9%, circulatory 9.9% (hypertension 8.9%), musculoskeletal 8.8%, infectious/parasitic 7.4%, endocrine/metabolic 6.5% (diabetes 5.1%), injuries 3.2%, anaemia 1.4%. In rural PHC data (Jaipur/Achrol RHTC, ~39,000 cases), the top four — URI/tonsillitis (21%), abdominal pain (17%), skin infections (15%), diarrhoea (13%) — made up two-thirds of load, followed by nutritional deficiency/anaemia (9.4%), bronchitis (7.8%), malaria (6.2%), eye disease (5.6%), ear infection/otitis media (2.7%), UTI (2%). A rural Puducherry PHC (68,818 episodes) ranked respiratory (26.2%) and musculoskeletal (26.0%) highest, then circulatory (10.9%), digestive (9.5%), injuries (7.6%).

**3. The MP tribal belt has a distinctive burden national averages hide.** In tribal Mandla (Prasad et al. household survey, 2019, N=741 ill persons), fever (60%) and cough/cold (36%) dominated, then body pain (14%), stomach pain (7%), weakness (5%), malaria (3%), diarrhoea (3%). Madhya Pradesh carries India's largest sickle cell disease load — per Colah et al. (*Indian Journal of Medical Research*, 2015), "Madhya Pradesh has the highest load with an estimated number of 9,61,492 sickle heterozygotes and 67,861 sickle homozygotes... 27 of the 45 districts in Madhya Pradesh fall under the sickle cell belt and the prevalence of HbS varies from 10 to 33 per cent," with Jhabua and Barwani ranked first and second by ST/SC population. Malaria in MP tribal districts is disproportionately *P. falciparum* and seasonal: per Sharma et al. (*IJMR*), districts with ≥30% tribal population "contributed to 46 per cent of total malaria cases, 70 per cent P. falciparum and 47 per cent malarial deaths in the country," and a 2024 Mandla situation analysis (*Frontiers in Public Health*) notes ~27 tribal-dominated districts (5% of the population) contribute ~44% of reported cases with 57.3% *P. falciparum*, peaking October–December. MP child anaemia (6–59 months) rose to 72.7% in NFHS-5 (2019–21), behind only Gujarat among large states, and tribal children are significantly more anaemic. This means malaria/fever, sickle-cell pain crisis, severe anaemia (pallor/breathlessness), and acute malnutrition must appear on the list even though a national-average list would rank them lower.

**4. Patients speak in idioms, not ICD.** Rural patients present with *bukhar* (fever), *khansi/sardi* (cough/cold), *badan dard* (body ache), *pet dard* (abdominal pain), *dast* (loose motions), *kamzori* (weakness), *chakkar* (dizziness), *ghabrahat* (palpitations/anxiety), *gas/acidity*, and vague self-reports like "sugar" or "BP." Some idioms carry no clean clinical mapping: "tension" in North India spans sadness, worry and somatic distress (Weaver, 2017); *garmi* ("heat") and *safed pani* (white discharge/leucorrhoea) are culturally loaded. Leucorrhoea is among the most common gynaecological complaints in Indian women — community prevalences range from 28.7% in rural Haryana (Singh, *Indian J Community Med*, 2007) up to 66.9% in a Mumbai urban slum (IJCMPH), with reproductive-age rates typically 27–30% — and is frequently an idiom for broader distress. Forcing a worker to convert *kamzori* into "anaemia" or *ghabrahat* into "anxiety" is a diagnostic act; the pick-list should record the idiom and defer the clinical judgment.

**5. Maternal and child clusters are already standardized.** IMNCI defines the child assessment cleanly: general danger signs (not able to drink/breastfeed, vomits everything, convulsions, lethargic/unconscious) plus four main symptoms — cough or difficult breathing, diarrhoea, fever, ear problem — plus checks for malnutrition and anaemia. Antenatal danger signs (India Safe Motherhood/WHO) are equally standard: vaginal bleeding, severe headache/blurred vision, convulsions, swelling of face/hands/feet, high fever, reduced/absent fetal movements, severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting.

**6. The right backbone is ICPC-2, localized.** ICPC-2 is the WHO-endorsed Reason-for-Encounter classification designed for exactly this — coding the patient's stated reason before diagnosis; it is biaxial (17 body-system chapters × components including symptoms/complaints) and already translated into 20+ languages. India's own tools cover overlapping ground: the ASHA CBAC checklist enumerates NCD/TB/screening symptoms (breathlessness, cough >2 weeks, blood in sputum, weight loss, mouth ulcers/patches, lumps, vision/hearing problems, PHQ-2 mood items); the Ayushman Bharat HWC 12-service package defines the service scope; WHO PEN covers hypertension/diabetes/CVD/respiratory. Recommendation: use ICPC-2 codes as the stored backbone, seed content from CBAC + IMNCI + ANC danger signs (India-specific and already in use), and validate frequency order against local registers.

## Details

### Frequency evidence base (what ranks where, and how reliable)
| Source | Setting | Top presenting complaints / morbidities |
|---|---|---|
| Kumar et al., ICPC-3 study, Odisha (N=3,044) | Public DH/CHC/PHC OPD | Fever, hypertension, abdominal pain, chest pain, arthritis, skin disease, cough, diabetes, injury; 65% presented a symptom not a diagnosis |
| AIIMS Bhopal CUHA (Gupta et al., N=6,685, 2014) | Central-India primary care | Respiratory 27.2% (ARI 23.9%), digestive 10.9%, circulatory 9.9% (HTN 8.9%), musculoskeletal 8.8%, infectious 7.4%, diabetes 5.1%, injury 3.2%, anaemia 1.4% |
| Jaipur RHTC (Achrol), Rajasthan (~39,000) | Rural PHC/RHTC OPD | URI/tonsillitis 21%, abdominal pain 17%, skin 15%, diarrhoea 13%, nutritional/anaemia 9.4%, bronchitis 7.8%, malaria 6.2%, eye 5.6%, otitis media 2.7%, UTI 2% |
| Rural Puducherry PHC (68,818) | Rural PHC OPD | Respiratory 26.2%, musculoskeletal 26.0%, circulatory 10.9%, digestive 9.5%, injuries 7.6% |
| Mandla tribal survey, MP (Prasad et al., N=741 ill) | Tribal household survey | Fever 60%, cough/cold 36%, body pain 14%, stomach pain 7%, weakness 5%, malaria 3%, diarrhoea 3% |
| Paediatric OPD (CUHA Bhopal) | Children <15 | Respiratory ~40–43% (mostly ARI), infectious/parasitic ~11–12%, digestive ~10%, injuries/wounds 4.6–7.6%, malnutrition 3–4% |

Paediatric context from the subagent review: fever is the single top paediatric presenting complaint (national estimates up to ~70% of paediatric OPD visits, ~22–27% as chief complaint), ARI drives 50–75% of febrile visits, and gastroenteritis/diarrhoea 10–25%.

Caveat on data quality: most Indian "morbidity" studies report *provisional diagnoses* from OPD registers, not the patient's presenting complaint verbatim — the ICPC studies (Odisha, and the Indian urban ICPC studies) are the closest to true reason-for-encounter data. Direct OPD-register presenting-complaint studies from the western MP tribal districts (Dhar/Jhabua/Barwani) with ranked percentages were not located; the Mandla data is a household self-report survey, and the Datia data (respiratory infection, superficial dermatophytosis, anaemia in women, otitis media, pneumonia as the leading records) is a district-hospital record review whose ranked percentages sit behind a paywall. This is a genuine local-evidence gap and is the strongest reason not to freeze the list before local validation.

### Why idiom-first wording matters (and the salience problem)
An ASHA, nurse, or compounder is not a diagnostician. When a patient says *kamzori* (weakness), the clinically correct target could be anaemia, hypoglycaemia, depression, post-viral fatigue, or simple exhaustion; when a patient says *garmi* it may mean burning urination, acidity, heat rash, or a culturally-framed state. If the pick-list only shows English clinical categories, the worker is forced to silently perform a differential — an error-prone clinical-salience judgment that corrupts both the immediate triage and the future training labels. The design fix: show the vernacular idiom as the primary label, store the clinical term and ICPC-2 code as metadata, allow multi-select, and never require the worker to "upgrade" an idiom into a diagnosis.

### Standard maternal/child cluster lists (map directly to items)
- **IMNCI general danger signs:** unable to drink/breastfeed; vomits everything; convulsions; lethargic/unconscious.
- **IMNCI main child symptoms:** cough/difficult breathing; diarrhoea; fever; ear problem; plus malnutrition and anaemia checks.
- **Antenatal danger signs:** vaginal bleeding; severe headache/blurred vision; convulsions; swelling face/hands; high fever; reduced fetal movements; severe abdominal pain; persistent vomiting.

## Recommended starting pick-list (~30 items)

Tiers: **VC** = very common, **C** = common, **RF** = red flag/danger sign (always present regardless of frequency).

### Adult general (very common / common)
1. Fever — *Bukhar* — VC — ICPC-2 A03
2. Cough / cold — *Khansi / sardi-zukam* — VC — R05/R74
3. Body ache / joint or back pain — *Badan dard / jodon-kamar dard* — VC — L01–L18 / A01
4. Abdominal pain — *Pet dard* — VC — D01/D06
5. Loose motions / diarrhoea — *Dast* — VC — D11
6. Weakness / tiredness — *Kamzori / thakan* — VC — A04
7. Headache — *Sar dard* — VC — N01
8. Gas / acidity / burning chest — *Gas / jalan / khatta* — VC — D02/D08
9. Dizziness / giddiness — *Chakkar* — C — N17
10. Vomiting / nausea — *Ulti / ji ghabrana* — C — D09/D10
11. Breathlessness — *Saans phoolna / dama* — C — R02
12. Skin problem — itching/rash/boils — *Khujli / phunsi / daane* — C — S06/S02
13. Eye problem — redness/pain/vision — *Aankh aana / dhundhla dikhna* — C — F05/F02
14. Ear problem — pain/discharge — *Kaan dard / kaan behna* — C — H01/H04
15. Painful/burning urination — *Peshab mein jalan* — C — U01/U71
16. Known "BP" / high blood pressure — *BP ki bimari* — C — K85/K86
17. Known "sugar" / diabetes — *Sugar / madhumeh* — C — T89/T90
18. Palpitations / anxiety-restlessness — *Ghabrahat / dhadkan* — C — K04/P01
19. Injury / wound / fall — *Chot / ghaav* — C — A80/S18
20. Toothache / mouth problem — *Daant dard / muh ka chaala* — C — D19/D82

### Region-specific endemic (must-include for MP tribal belt)
21. Fever with chills / suspected malaria — *Thand-kãpan wala bukhar* — VC (local) — A73
22. Severe body/bone pain crisis (sickle cell) — *Haddi/badan mein tez dard* — C (local, endemic) — A05 / sickle-cell metadata
23. Pallor / severe weakness / suspected anaemia — *Khoon ki kami / peelapan* — C (local) — B80/B82
24. Not eating / weight loss / child not growing — *Bhookh na lagna / bachcha kamzor* — C (local) — T05/T08
25. Cough > 2 weeks / suspected TB — *2 hafte se zyada khansi* — C — A70/R05 (CBAC flag)
26. White discharge (women) — *Safed pani* — C — X14

### Maternal / child
27. Pregnancy-related complaint or ANC visit — *Garbhavastha / jaanch* — C — W78/W84
28. Child: fever/cough/fast breathing/diarrhoea — *Bachche ko bukhar/khansi/dast* — VC — A03/R05/D11

### Always-present red flags (danger signs)
29. Pregnancy danger sign — bleeding, fits, severe headache/blurred vision, swelling, reduced fetal movement — *Khoon aana / daura / soojan / bachche ka hilna band* — RF — W-code cluster
30. Child danger sign — not drinking/breastfeeding, vomits everything, convulsions, lethargic/unconscious — RF — IMNCI cluster
31. Adult emergency — chest pain, severe breathlessness, unconsciousness/fits, snake/scorpion bite, severe bleeding, heavy trauma — *Seene mein dard / behoshi / saanp-bichhoo kaatna* — RF — A80/K01/R02/A88
32. **Other — please describe** (mandatory free-text → routes to human-read narrative)

### How to handle the long tail
Never force a tick. The "Other — describe" item must be a first-class option that opens a free-text/voice field, flags the record for a human (CHO/MO) to read, and is logged as unlabelled data (not fed to the classifier as a positive label for any existing class). Periodically mine "Other" entries: any idiom appearing repeatedly is a candidate new pick-list item — this is how the list should grow, driven by local data rather than a priori taxonomy.

## Recommendations
1. **Ship the ~30-item list above as v0.1, explicitly labelled a hypothesis, not a standard.** Wire every item to an ICPC-2 code in metadata from day one so downstream analytics and any future model training are standards-aligned.
2. **Validate against real local registers before wide deployment (hard gate).** Pull 4–8 weeks of OPD, ANC, and child-health register data from 3–5 PHCs/sub-centres in Dhar/Jhabua/Barwani, tally actual presenting complaints, and re-rank. Benchmarks to change the list: any complaint appearing in >5% of local visits that is missing must be added; any tier-VC item appearing in <2% locally should be demoted. This step is non-negotiable because no ranked OPD presenting-complaint dataset exists for these specific districts.
3. **Run a worker-comprehension test in Malvi and Hindi.** Have ASHAs/nurses map 50 real patient utterances to the list; if inter-rater agreement is low on any item, the wording is wrong — fix the vernacular label, not the worker.
4. **Keep idiom-first labels and multi-select.** Store idiom + clinical term + ICPC-2 code as a triple. Do not collapse *kamzori*, *chakkar*, and "anaemia" into one clinical bucket at entry time.
5. **Protect the training-label pipeline.** Treat pick-list ticks as *presenting complaints*, never as diagnoses; hold "Other" text out of the positive-label set; have a clinician periodically audit a sample of tick→outcome pairs before any complaint→condition model is trained.
6. **Seasonally surface malaria/fever and dengue items** during monsoon (July) and the October–December *P. falciparum* peak, when MP tribal-belt transmission spikes.

## Caveats
- **Local frequency data is genuinely thin.** No peer-reviewed rural PHC/sub-centre OPD register study with ranked presenting-complaint percentages was found for Dhar, Jhabua, or Barwani specifically. The MP figures used here come from AIIMS Bhopal (urban-adjacent primary care), a Mandla *household* survey, and a Datia *district-hospital* record review — all imperfect proxies. Treat national/other-state rankings as priors to be overwritten by local data.
- **Diagnosis ≠ presenting complaint.** Most Indian morbidity studies code provisional diagnoses from registers, not the patient's own words; the true reason-for-encounter distribution in these districts is unmeasured.
- **Idioms are unstable and gendered.** "Tension," *garmi*, and *safed pani* carry meanings that shift by community and gender; some (e.g., leucorrhoea/white discharge) are weakly correlated with organic disease and often signal psychosocial distress. Do not hard-map them to organic diagnoses.
- **Sickle cell and anaemia items are salience calls.** A worker cannot know a pain crisis is sickle-cell; the item is worded as a presenting symptom (severe bone/body pain, pallor) with the clinical hypothesis kept as metadata for the MO.
- **Western primary-care vocabularies (ICPC-2, BEACH-style rankings) are structurally useful but epidemiologically foreign** — their frequency weights reflect high-income practice and should not be used to order the Indian list; only their coding structure should be borrowed.
